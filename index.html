How this is worked out — and what it can't tell you

The number

For each pack slot the model holds an expected number of cards per pack for each rarity. Value is the sum over all of them:

EV = Σ (cards per pack × average market price of the cards that slot can give you)

Expectation adds up regardless of how the slots actually interact, so writing the odds this way keeps the sum exact without having to guess the pack's internal wiring. It also matches how community data gets reported — "1 in 13 packs" is just 0.077 per pack.

Where the prices come from

  • Every card in every set, at its TCGplayer market price, Near Mint.
  • Pokémon prices come from TCGplayer's price guide, which names each printing, so a reverse holo is priced as a reverse holo and not as its plain version.
  • One Piece needs no split — each rarity there exists in exactly one printing.
  • Riftbound commons and uncommons are priced separately as normal and foil, because the pack has a dedicated foil slot and the premium is large.
  • Alternate arts are kept apart from their plain prints. They share a rarity on TCGplayer, so averaging them together would badly distort the result.

Where the odds come from

No publisher releases pull rates — not The Pokémon Company, not Bandai, not Riot. What exists is counted packs, and how much has been counted differs sharply by game:

  • Pokémon. TCGplayer's Authentication Center opens several thousand packs before a set lands and publishes the per-rarity odds with confidence intervals. 22 sets use those counts, each linked to its own study. The rest fall back to an era template.
  • Riftbound. Origins, Unleashed and Vendetta have release-window counts of their own. Spiritforged has none and borrows from its neighbours.
  • One Piece. Nothing per-set exists. Every source publishes one set of odds covering the whole game, so that is what every One Piece set here uses, and none of them claim to be measured.

A measured badge means that set was counted; estimated means the odds were borrowed from neighbouring sets; thin means something else is wrong — the set is too new for its prices to have settled, it doesn't follow its era's structure, or even the study left a tier unmeasured. Four reasons not to trust EV alone

  • The average is not the typical pack. Value sits in cards you will almost never pull. The chase share column shows how much of the EV rides on rarities appearing less than once in 20 packs. Where that share is high, most packs come in far under EV and a rare few carry everything.
  • Market price is not what you get. Selling means fees, shipping, time and undercutting. The fees switch takes 13.25% off, which is still optimistic.
  • Prices fall as a set is opened. A new set's EV is measured against prices that the opening itself pushes down.
  • Sealed vintage is not an EV play. Old packs trade on being sealed, not on their contents, so a deeply negative return there is the right answer.

Assumptions worth knowing

  • Within a rarity, every card is assumed equally likely. Real sets sometimes short-print.
  • Box EV is pack EV times the pack count. Box toppers and promos aren't counted.
  • Cards with no market price yet are valued at their rarity's average rather than zero; the coverage figure in each open row shows how much of the pool that affects.
  • Energy, token, rune and code cards are treated as worthless.
